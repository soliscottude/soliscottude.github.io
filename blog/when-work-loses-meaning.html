Responsible Leadership

When Work Loses Its Meaning

AI, leadership, and the future of young workers in New Zealand.

By Scott Yang May 2026

I spent years learning to code because I believed it would lead somewhere.

Seven years ago, I started studying Computer and Information Sciences at AUT. Late nights debugging. Tutorials that made no sense until they suddenly did. Database projects, mobile app development, web applications, programming assignments, and countless hours spent debugging code that refused to work.

Then AI arrived.

That very first time I tried using it to code something, I couldn't believe how quickly it worked. All those hours of searching, debugging, and testing something, all that work was done within minutes.

The excitement wore off fast. If AI could already do parts of the job I had spent years learning to do, what would happen to people like me who were still trying to get their first opportunity?

That question stayed with me. But the more I thought about it, the more I realised that AI isn't the real problem.

Technology has always changed how people work. This is just the latest version of that.

AI cannot make decisions. AI doesn't decide to stop hiring graduates, and it doesn't decide to cut training budgets.

People do.

Well, actually, leaders do.

The Jobs Are Changing, And Not Everyone Is Ready

AI is already changing things in New Zealand workplaces, and if you're a student or recent graduate, you've probably felt it. A Massey University study covered by the NZ Herald found generative AI hitting entry-level creative roles hard. Back in 2023, RNZ was already asking how much AI would change the way New Zealanders work. Two years later, we have a clearer answer: job hunters and recruiters are both struggling, and entry-level hiring is slowing down.

This isn't a distant future problem. It's already here. Look around campus or scroll through LinkedIn and you'll see it for yourself. It's happening right now, to students and graduates looking for their first real job.

And the people most affected aren't the experienced ones. It's students and graduates. People at the beginning of their careers who need entry-level work to build skills and confidence, and are finding fewer doors open.

Nobody starts as a senior developer. People become experienced because someone, at some point, was willing to invest in them as beginners. If AI removes those entry points, the experience gap doesn't just affect individuals, it affects entire generations entering the workforce.

And beyond the economics, there's something harder to measure: what this does to people's sense of meaning. Work isn't just income. It's identity, growth, belonging, and purpose. When those are threatened, the anxiety that follows is real, and it's showing up in the conversations happening right now among students and young professionals across New Zealand.

The Real Cost of Moving Fast

Most organisations aren't trying to hurt anyone. Staying competitive is hard, costs are real, and if something gets the job done faster, of course you're going to use it.

But here's the problem. When every decision is filtered through the question "how do we improve productivity?", and not "what does this mean for our people?", something important gets left out.

Most organisations are running on a simple equation, efficiency up, costs down. Leadership researchers call this a transactional mindset, and on its own, it's not necessarily bad. But when it becomes the only lens through which decisions are made, it's easy to lose sight of what work actually means to the people doing it.

Even New Zealand's own AI strategy reflects this tension. RNZ's analysis noted that the strategy is strong on economic opportunity but much weaker on addressing the ethical and social risks of AI. That gap matters.

When organisations automate away the roles that once helped graduates and junior employees gain experience, they're not just reducing costs. They're removing opportunities. And when enough of those opportunities disappear, the effects don't stop with today's job seekers. They shape the future workforce as well.

Why Leaders Don't Just "Fix" This

Most leaders aren't bad or uncaring people. They're caught inside the same system they're helping to build, and that's what makes this so hard to untangle. When shareholders want returns and boards want results, the pressure doesn't really leave room for asking whether any of this is actually good for the people involved. Moving fast isn't just rewarded. Slowing down to think about people can feel like a risk nobody wants to take.

This is where responsible leadership matters. Not as an abstract concept, but as a practical question: who is actually responsible for what happens to people when technology changes the rules? Maak and Pless (2006) argue that leaders have obligations not just to shareholders, but to everyone affected by their decisions. Leaders who adopt AI without asking that question aren't just making a business decision, they're making a human one, whether they realise it or not.

The problem is that the system itself isn't built around those questions. It's built around speed, output, and growth. Everything else, the human stuff, just doesn't show up in the metrics. Wellbeing doesn't show up in a KPI. Long-term trust doesn't appear on a balance sheet. So they get left out of the decision.

The challenge isn't just finding better individual leaders. It's changing the systems that shape what "good leadership" looks like in the first place.

What I Would Do Differently

I've thought a lot about what kind of leader I want to be. Not in an abstract way, more like, what would I actually do differently if I were in that position?

After graduating, working at Workday, and now coming back to study, I know first-hand how uncertain this all feels from the inside. It's just context for why this issue isn't theoretical to me.

If I were leading an organisation right now, I wouldn't be reaching for AI as a way to trim headcount. I'd be asking how it could make the actual experience of work better, less grinding, more meaningful. And I'd be honest with people about what was changing before they found out through a redundancy email.

The part that frustrates me most is what happens to entry-level roles. Those jobs aren't just cheap labour used to fill gaps. They're how people build the experience that eventually makes them valuable. Cut those, and you're not just saving money in the short term. You're quietly defunding the next generation of your own workforce.

A Different Question Altogether

Most of the conversation around AI in New Zealand is framed around economics. How do we stay competitive? How do we boost productivity? It's a useful question, but it's not the only one worth asking.

Māori leadership starts from a different place entirely. The concept of manaakitanga, the responsibility to care for people's dignity and wellbeing, isn't just a nice value to display on a company website. Applied to AI adoption, it asks something concrete: are we implementing this technology in a way that looks after the people affected by it? Not eventually. Now.

Kaitiakitanga pushes the question further. A kaitiaki doesn't make decisions based only on what works today. They think about what they're leaving behind. For an organisation rolling out AI, that means asking whether they're protecting the entry-level roles and learning opportunities that the next generation depends on, or quietly removing them in the name of efficiency.

This isn't just theory. Te Mana Raraunga, the Māori Data Sovereignty Network, has been pushing these exact questions in New Zealand, not just whether AI works, but whether it works for the people it affects, and who actually gets a say in that. According to Katene (2010), manaakitanga isn't just a value, it's something a good leader is actually expected to live by, including making sure the people around them are looked after and have room to grow. That's not something you see on many AI strategy documents.

What You Can Do Right Now

AI is coming. That part isn't really up for debate. But what it does to people, and who ends up better or worse off because of it, that's still being decided. And it's being decided by people, not algorithms.

There are a lot of people still waiting for their first real shot. Whether they get one depends on the choices being made right now.

If you're still studying or just starting out, ask employers that question directly. How are you using AI, and what does that mean for someone trying to get their first real opportunity? See what they say. The New Zealand government has guidance on responsible AI use if you need somewhere to start.

And if you're the one making those calls, ask yourself whether you're creating opportunities or quietly removing them.

What gets decided now is going to stick. Not just for today's job market, but for what work actually means to the next generation.
