Responsible Leadership

When Work Loses Its Meaning

AI, Leadership, and the Future of Young Workers in New Zealand.

By Scott Yang May 2026

I spent years learning to code and I was hoping it would help me get a stable job.

Seven years ago, I started studying Computer and Information Sciences at AUT. Late nights debugging. Tutorials that made no sense until they suddenly did. Database projects, mobile app development, web applications, programming assignments, and countless hours spent debugging code that refused to work.

Then AI arrived.

That very first time I tried using it to code something, I couldn't believe how quickly it worked. All those hours of searching, debugging, and testing something, all that work was done within minutes.

The excitement wore off fast. If AI could already do parts of the job I had spent years learning to do, what would happen to people like me who were still trying to get their first opportunity?

That question stayed with me. But the more I thought about it, the more I realised that AI isn't the real problem.

Technology has always changed how people work. This is just the latest version of that.

AI doesn't decide who gets a chance to start a career. It doesn't decide whether a graduate gets hired or whether a training budget survives another round of cost-cutting.

People make those decisions. Well actually, leaders do.

The Jobs Are Changing, And Not Everyone Is Ready

AI is already changing things in New Zealand workplaces, most people have already felt it. A Massey University study found generative AI hitting entry-level creative roles hard. RNZ reported that job hunters and recruiters are both struggling, and entry-level hiring is slowing down. The New Zealand government also has put out guidance on what responsible AI use is supposed to look like.

This isn’t a distant future problem. It’s already here. Look around campus or scroll through LinkedIn and you'll see it for yourself. It's happening right now, to students and graduates looking for their first real job.

I know this because I am experiencing it. After graduating from AUT, I spent months sending out applications, I only got a few rejections without any explanation. I started questioning myself about my skillsets and abilities, did I just spent several years learning the wrong things?

When I finally got a position at Workday, I was overwhelmed at first, everything is strange to me, new systems, new processes, I didn’t know how to handle them. But I had the chance to learn. That chance is important to me, especially I hardly have any working experience. If AI had already replaced those kinds of entry-level positions, I wouldn’t have had it.

At Workday, I started noticing that AI was getting better at those kinds of tasks I had spent time learning. I wasn’t just worried about my job. I was wondering if the skills I had built are still valuable. That kind of uncertainty and stress make you question your direction.

The Real Cost of Moving Fast

For organisations, staying competitive is difficult, they have to concern about costs, and if a tool can improve efficiency, embracing it is the right decision.

But when every decision depends on cutting costs and moving faster, no one would think about what does this mean for the people involved?

There’s even a name for this kind of thinking. Researchers call it transactional leadership, basically, you hit your targets, I hit mine, everyone goes home. And honestly, that works fine for a lot of things. The problem is when it becomes the only way anyone thinks. At some point, the question “what does this mean for the people doing the work” just stops getting asked.

New Zealand’s own AI strategy does the same thing. RNZ's analysis found it’s full of talk about economic opportunity, but hardly any on what happens to people when things go wrong. If even the national strategy skips that part, it’s not surprising that individual organisations do too.

I saw this up close at Workday. Sometimes after we’d already locked in a migration date with a customer, the internal team had to change it. Last minute, valid reasons, but the customer didn’t know any of that. They just knew the date they’d planned around was gone. I was the one who had to call them. It was awkward. And it made me realise something: when a decision gets made without much thought for the person on the other end, someone still has to deal with the reaction. It’s usually not the person who made the decision.

Why Leaders Don't Just "Fix" This

I don't think most leaders tend to make things harder for people.

Maak and Pless (2006) say leaders owe something to everyone their decisions touch, not just shareholders. I think about that when I see AI rollouts announced in a press release while the people affected find out later. You made a choice. You just didn’t own it.

The system doesn’t make it easy either. Wellbeing doesn’t show up in a KPI. Trust doesn’t appear on a balance sheet. So they get left out, it’s not because nobody cares, but because nobody saved space for them.

Finding a better individual to put in charge doesn’t fix any of this. The system just shapes them the same way it shaped everyone else.

What I Would Do Differently

After I graduated, I applied for hundreds of different positions. Most of them just disappeared. No reply, no feedback. I didn't know if my CV was wrong, if I was applying for the wrong positions, or if I was just not good enough. That uncertainty is stressing me in a hard way and I couldn’t sleep well. At Workday, when I had to call a customer and tell them the migration date had changed, I didn’t send a template. I actually explained what happened. Because I remembered what it felt like when nobody explained anything to me.

I’m not in a leadership position yet. But I know what I’d care about. At Workday I used AI to work through cases faster, and that made the job less overwhelming, not because I was doing less but because I had more room to actually think. That’s the version worth building toward. And if something was changing for my team, I’d want them to hear it from me first.

There’s something I keep thinking about. I spent two years studying civil engineering in China without really knowing why. It felt like the safe choice. Class, assignments, exams, repeat. I wasn’t going anywhere, I was just repeating same schedule every day. Choosing to study for IELTS, to actually leave, to switch to something I wanted , that changed things. Not immediately, but it did. That kind of shift needs time and room. Someone who heard too early that the doors were already closing might not have tried at all.

A Different Question Altogether

Most of the conversation about AI in New Zealand is about the same things. Growth. Staying competitive. Getting there faster. At Workday, the projects that actually went well weren’t the ones that just hit their technical targets. They were the ones where someone had stopped to think about what the experience felt like on the other end.

Māori leadership asks a different question to begin with. Manaakitanga , care, respect, genuine responsibility for others , isn’t something you file under company values. It’s something you either do or you don’t, every time a decision affects someone. That’s a different standard than most AI strategies are held to.

Kaitiakitanga is about what you’re leaving behind. A kaitiaki isn’t thinking about this quarter. They’re thinking about what the people after them are going to find. I think about that when I see entry-level roles quietly disappear. Someone made that call. They probably had reasons. The next generation just has to live with it.

Te Mana Raraunga has been on this for years , asking not just whether AI performs well technically, but whether it actually serves the people it’s supposed to help, and who even gets a voice in that conversation. Katene (2010) makes a point I think about a lot: manaakitanga isn’t an aspiration. It’s how you’re judged. You look after the people around you and give them room to grow, or you don’t. I’ve read a lot of AI strategy documents. That question is not in most of them.

What You Can Do Right Now

The era of AI has arrived, but it doesn’t affect what it does to people, cause that part is still being decided by humans, not technology.

Looking back, I've changed direction more than once. I studied civil engineering. Then software development. Then business management. I changed direction more than once and each time I had no idea if it would work out. Civil engineering to computer science. Workday to business school. What kept me going was that the next door wasn't locked yet. I hope the people starting out now still have that. I'm just not sure anyone is making sure of it.
